The news: the sector's cost-curve story rests on one vehicle that discloses its marginal cost. Measured against the right denominator, that single point goes the wrong way — cost per launch fell 12.0% while cost per kilogram rose 32.0%. The two numbers are the same fact.

Why that is not a rounding argument. Electron flew six missions in Q2 2026 and two of them were suborbital testbeds carrying zero kilograms to orbit. Cost per launch divides by six; cost per kilogram divides by four. 0.88 × 1.50 = 1.32 exactly — the inversion is arithmetic, not interpretation.

What this means for the programme. Any thesis pricing a launch-cost decline into a revenue model is pricing a number that has not appeared. The decline is real in the denominator everyone quotes and absent in the denominator that matters. That is the widest-reaching result here.

The second finding is about ambiguity, and it is five times larger. SPCX's 8-K prints four numerators and two denominators on one page; the four cost-per-kilogram values that follow span 12.8× and none of them is a calculation error. The spread decomposes exactly — numerator 1.617×, denominator 7.886×.

What it does not mean. The value pool did not vanish, it moved. Two acquirers bought the customer relationship underneath two independent operators within twelve months, and neither paid for the margin. Anchor (a) holds; anchor (b) is falsified.

How to read this report. The one measured point comes first, then the denominator argument that generalises it, then where the money actually went, then the register — which was itself defeated by one of the defects it classifies, inside an artifact written to document it, with all three automated gates passing. The recommendation is not a trade. This thesis produces no positions by design. It produces a citation rule: carry the basis with the grade.

The Inversion · Cost per Launch Against Cost per Kilogram

Cost per launch fell while cost per kilogram rose

Same vehicle, same quarter, same filings. One denominator moved down and the other moved up, and the gap between them is the HASTE mission mix.

The identity is exact. 0.88 × 1.50 = 1.32. Six missions to four is a factor of 1.50; the cost line fell to 0.88 of its prior level; the product is the 1.32 increase in cost per kilogram. Nothing here is estimated. Both the cost line and the launch counts are filed — RKLB 10-Q p.6.

Why the correction is not optional. A suborbital testbed that reaches no orbit delivers no kilograms. Counting it in the denominator of a cost-per-kilogram metric makes the metric move for a reason that has nothing to do with cost per kilogram. The uncorrected series would have shown a decline.

What this means. If the sector's productivity claim is true, this is the one place it should be visible, and it is not. That does not prove the claim false for Starship or Neutron — it proves the claim has never been tested where it is testable, which is the narrower and more uncomfortable finding.

The spread is almost entirely denominator. Numerator factor 1,581 / 978 = 1.617×; denominator factor 1,041 / 132 = 7.886×; product 12.75×. The ambiguity is five times larger on the axis nobody argues about.

The boundary that makes the numerator ambiguous is filed, and it is the customer boundary. What this means. The spread will be read as uncertainty when it is actually definition. A reader who treats it as noise will average four values that are not commensurable — which is why the ladder, not a single number, is the deliverable.

Neutron · A Case That Closes on One Basis and Fires on Another

Neutron closes on one basis and fires on another

PIL-4 registered a threshold for Neutron's implied cost per kilogram. The registered number was wrong, and the correction changes the verdict.

The registered figure was an Electron price divided by a Neutron denominator — a DA-30 basis collapse inside the definition of the test itself. Corrected, Neutron's implied cost of $3,846–$4,231/kg sits below the band, so the falsifier does not fire on the registered bar (RKLB 10-K p.11).

But the same case fires on two other bases. Neutron is unflown: its cost is a model over an assumed payload, and its A′ breakeven payloads are 11,851 kg at a $50M price and 13,035 kg at $55M — both above the vehicle's stated capability. The case closes on the registered bar and is knife-edge on a third.

What this means. A threshold derived from a price and a cost is not a threshold — it is two questions. Neutron's verdict is conditional on a basis that has never flown, and any model that treats its cost per kilogram as an input is treating a target as a measurement.

The Register · A Defect That Defeated Its Own Documentation

The register that classifies defects was defeated by one

Data-integrity entry DA-23 records that the platform serves a filed negative as a positive of identical magnitude. It is confirmed at 9 of 9 tickers in this universe.

The sharpest instance is not an issuer. It is an artifact. The GSAT analysis written to document DA-23 was itself reproducing it on eight figures — including a claimed divergence that existed only because the artifact had read a stripped value.

All three automated gates passed on the stripped version. A clean gate result is not evidence of a clean number — because the gate and the number were computed the same way.

The pair that shows the mechanism in one line. RKLB's served operating income is +57,514,000. The filed figure is −57,514 in thousands. Same digits, opposite sign, and a scale factor that hides it. Sold as a positive, the loss reads as a gain.

The census of the defect, by issuer. PL 33 of 33 filed operating facts stripped (PL 10-Q p.24); FLY 15 of 15; GSAT 13 confirmed instances with 16 unexercised (GSAT p.27). Where the test could not run, this thesis records it as UNEXERCISED — not as clean.

What this means. Every figure a downstream thesis inherits should carry the basis it was read on. A number that reproduces arithmetically can still answer a different question than its label names — and no check that recomputes the same way will catch it.
